Nepal Hazard Watch, methodology notes

How this app actually decides a lake is risky

A plain walkthrough of the scoring formula, where every input comes from, the assumptions built into it, what it cannot claim yet, and what could be built next.

Last updated September 8, 2026. Covers 97 glacial lakes and 123 glacier watch points across Nepal (220 monitored points total).

A risk score with no explanation behind it is just a number to trust blindly, and that is not good enough for something people might actually act on. If a lake shows up under Watch on the dashboard, this page is where the reasoning lives: which real dataset fed into that decision, how old the data is allowed to be before it stops counting, and where the app is still guessing. Nothing here is written to sound more certain than the underlying data actually supports.

The short version

Every monitored lake and glacier watch point gets a composite score from 0 to 100, rebuilt every 30 minutes. It is built from six weighted pieces, each tied to a real, independently checkable dataset (USGS, n.d.; ICIMOD, n.d.; RGI Consortium, 2023; OpenWeather Ltd., n.d.; Copernicus, n.d.; Rawlins et al., 2025):

Equation 1.

score = 17 × earthquake_hazard
   + 21.25 × landslide_hazard
   + 21.25 × rainfall_hazard
   + 17 × lake_type_or_terrain_steepness
   + 8.5 × seasonal_factor
   + 15 × satellite_hazard

Each hazard term on the right is itself normalized to a range from 0 to 1 before the weight is applied, so the six weights above are a direct partition of the full 100-point scale. They are not round numbers because this is a six-factor formula built by extending an earlier five-factor one (20, 25, 25, 20, 10) once satellite tracking became reliable enough to weight in. Each of the original five was scaled down by a constant factor of 0.85 to free up exactly 15 points for the new satellite term, rather than simply adding 15 points on top of an already-full 100-point scale. Table 1 and Figure 1 show the resulting composition. The glacier formula uses the same six weights, substituting terrain steepness for lake type and an ice-cover signal for lake growth (both described below).

Where each number actually comes from

Earthquake hazard, 17% of the score

The app queries the USGS earthquake catalog every 10 minutes across several regions covering Nepal, pulling everything magnitude 4.0 and above within a 220 kilometer radius (USGS, n.d.). The magnitude 4.0 floor is not an arbitrary round number: Keefer's (1984) landmark study of 40 historical earthquakes found that magnitude 4.0 is approximately the minimum threshold below which an earthquake essentially never triggers landslides at all, with the maximum area capable of being affected growing from near zero at M4.0 up to hundreds of thousands of square kilometers for the largest recorded events. The 220 kilometer search radius is set to comfortably cover Keefer's magnitude-distance envelope for the range of earthquakes that actually occur near Nepal, rather than a number pulled from nowhere.

For each lake, every quake from the last 30 days gets turned into a single hazard value from magnitude, distance, and depth using an exponential decay curve, so a bigger, closer, shallower quake counts for more and the effect fades out smoothly rather than cutting off at some arbitrary line. There is deliberately no hard floor on earthquakes the way there is on landslides. Shaking is a genuinely regional phenomenon, felt over a wide area rather than confined to one valley, so letting it decay naturally seemed more honest than drawing an artificial boundary around it.

The 30 day window also decays with the quake's own age now, not just distance and depth. That change came out of actually reading the aftershock literature rather than assuming a flat window was fine. Aftershock frequency follows what seismologists call the Omori law, roughly a 1 over time decay, and how long that decay runs depends on the size of the mainshock. A magnitude 5 quake's aftershocks mostly die out within days to weeks, but a magnitude 7 event, the scale of the real 2015 Gorkha earthquake, can keep producing meaningful aftershock activity for months. The Gorkha earthquake's own aftermath makes the point concretely: Kargel et al. (2015) mapped over 4,000 quake-triggered landslides across the region and found the fractured slopes left behind kept producing rockfalls and avalanches well beyond the earthquake itself, and separate reporting traced a real GLOF in the Bhotekoshi and Sunkoshi valleys in July 2016, over a year later, back to sediment loosened by that same earthquake. A flat 30 day cutoff could not tell the difference between a quake from yesterday and one from 29 days ago, and then dropped it to zero entirely at day 30. Now the hazard value from a single quake fades exponentially with a 12 day half-decay constant: a quake is down to about 37 percent of its fresh value after 12 days, and about 8 percent after 30, rather than sitting at full strength the whole time and then vanishing. Twelve days is a deliberate middle ground, chosen by hand rather than fit to any specific magnitude's aftershock curve, stated here plainly instead of presented as more precise than it is.

Landslide hazard, 21.25% of the score

USGS's own seismic network classifies some detections as landslide type events rather than ordinary tectonic earthquakes, and that classification is treated as a meaningfully stronger signal, since it is a direct sighting of actual mass movement rather than shaking that might or might not destabilize something later (USGS, n.d.). This is the one component scoped tightly on purpose: a landslide only counts if it happened in the last 7 days, within 30 kilometers, and in the same river basin as the lake. Outside that window it contributes exactly zero, not a small leftover fraction of a point. The 30 kilometer radius and 7 day window themselves are a practical scoping decision, not derived from a specific published study, and are labeled as such in the sourcing table further down this page.

That tight scoping came out of a real mistake caught during testing. Earlier, landslide hazard decayed smoothly over 30 days and 100 kilometers, the same way earthquake hazard does. In practice that meant a single real landslide could quietly push dozens of lakes across a huge stretch of the country into Watch for weeks, with no connection to whether those specific lakes were actually near it. Once the numbers were checked against the real database, roughly a third of everything on the dashboard was sitting under Watch purely because of that one event. The fix requires both proximity and a shared river basin, matched against a small curated list of towns per basin rather than real hydrological boundary data, since watershed polygons for these rivers are not available. It is a reasonable stand in, not a survey grade measurement, and that distinction is worth being clear about rather than letting it pass as more precise than it is.

Because this floor has a hard 7-day expiry, it also decays away on its own once the underlying event ages out, with no manual reset needed. As of September 7, 2026, the one glacier watch point that had been sitting under Watch purely on this floor returned to Normal once its triggering landslide detection passed the 7-day mark. It is a small but real illustration that the alert reflects a currently active signal, not a fact that gets stuck on the dashboard indefinitely.

Rainfall hazard, 21.25% of the score

Live weather comes from OpenWeatherMap, refreshed every 30 minutes for every monitored point (OpenWeather Ltd., n.d.). Three windows get tracked at once: the last 24 hours, the last 7 days, and the last 14 days. A single unusually wet day can push the score up on its own, but so can two straight weeks of steady, moderate rain even if no single day looked dramatic, because sustained ground saturation is a real slope stability hazard in its own right, independent of any one day's total. The rainfall signal is also dampened when the temperature is below freezing, since precipitation at that point falls as snow rather than the kind of rain that drives glacial melt and runoff.

The daily rainfall figures used here (25mm marks conditions as ELEVATED, 50mm as HEAVY) are informed by, but deliberately set well below, the empirical rainfall-landslide threshold Dahal and Hasegawa (2008) established for the Nepal Himalaya specifically: their analysis of 193 rainfall-triggered landslides across the country found daily totals above roughly 144mm meaningfully raise landslide risk. That figure is a threshold for actual landslide initiation. The lower thresholds used here are an earlier warning stage, meant to flag conditions worth watching well before rainfall reaches the point Dahal and Hasegawa found associated with real slope failure, not an attempt to reproduce their number directly.

Lake type or terrain steepness, 17% of the score

For an actual lake, this weight comes directly from ICIMOD's own risk classification, Very High, High, Medium, or Low, which already accounts for things like the lake's damming mechanism and documented history that this app makes no attempt to recompute (ICIMOD, n.d.). For a glacier watch point that has no lake yet, the real terrain slope from the Randolph Glacier Inventory is substituted instead (RGI Consortium, 2023). The reasoning is that a steep, unstable glacier terminus sitting close to a river can collapse and dam that river directly, without ever forming a lake first, which is exactly what happened near Langtang Lirung in August 2026.

The slope range used to turn steepness into a hazard value (0 below 20 degrees, ramping up to a full 1.0 at 70 degrees) tracks the same pattern found in Himalayan landslide susceptibility studies. Jasrotia et al. (2026), studying the northwestern Himalaya, classified slopes above 45 degrees as highly susceptible to landslides, with susceptibility already rising through the 15 to 45 degree range. The 20 degree floor used here sits at the low end of that rising range, and the 40 degree cutoff used for the separate landslide pre-condition flag below sits just under their 45 degree high-susceptibility mark.

Seasonal factor, 8.5% of the score

Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology reports a normal monsoon onset of June 13 and withdrawal of October 2 (DHM, n.d.), and documented glacial lake failures cluster heavily inside that window. The seasonal effect is modeled as a smooth curve that peaks around the end of July and tapers down to zero by late October, matching that real withdrawal date, and again by early May, rather than a blunt switch that treats every monsoon day identically and every other day as irrelevant. It is a real, well documented seasonal pattern grounded in Nepal's own official climatology, but it is still a calendar based approximation applied the same way to every lake in the country, not a location specific climate model, and real onset dates do shift year to year.

Satellite lake growth, 15% of the score Calibrated against real data

Every 5 days or so, matching Sentinel-2's real revisit cadence over Nepal, a small bounding box (about 1.3km across) around each lake's coordinates gets pulled through the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem's Statistics API, and the Normalized Difference Water Index is computed: NDWI = (green − NIR) / (green + NIR), the index McFeeters (1996) originally defined for delineating open water in satellite imagery, masking out cloud, shadow, and cirrus pixels using the scene classification layer (Copernicus, n.d.). McFeeters set zero as his original classification boundary, but noted that practical applications commonly shift the cutoff up into the 0 to 0.1 range to reduce false positives from mixed and turbid pixels; this app uses 0.1, the upper end of that commonly used range. That produces a water-fraction reading for the box at each real satellite pass, stored so the two most recent readings can be compared.

The comparison is not a raw before and after percentage jump. The relative change between two readings gets converted into an annualized rate, then graded against real growth-rate statistics from the Glacial Lake Observatory (GLO) dataset: 4,150 Himalayan and transboundary lakes tracked via Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 from 2017 to 2024, with statistically significance-tested expansion rates (Rawlins et al., 2025). Their statistically significant lakes averaged about 2.7%/year growth (median 1.9%), reaching roughly 9%/year at the 90th percentile and 18%/year at the extreme. Their non-significant, background-noise lakes topped out around 1.8 to 2.9%/year at the 90th percentile. That gap is what the thresholds below are built on. Below about 2%/year, relative growth is indistinguishable from ordinary measurement noise in that real dataset. Above about 18%/year is about as dramatic as any real lake in it got.

Satellite glacier ice-cover sudden drop, 15% of the score Provisional, not yet calibrated

The same satellite pipeline also tracks glacier termini, using a different index: NDSI = (green − SWIR1) / (green + SWIR1), the standard formulation used in operational snow-cover classification (e.g. Hall & Riggs, 2007), with a pixel counted as snow/ice above the conventional NDSI 0.4 threshold. Unlike lake growth, this is deliberately not framed as a slow annual trend. A real terminus collapse, like Langtang Lirung in August 2026, is a fast structural event measured in hours to days, not a gradual seasonal one, so the two most recent readings are compared directly as an absolute percentage-point drop in ice/snow-cover fraction, and the comparison only counts as "sudden" if the two readings are no more than 15 days apart.

September 7, 2026 was chosen as the baseline for every glacier rather than reaching back into the satellite archive the way the lake growth comparison does. Lake growth is a slow trend worth comparing against an older reading. A collapse is not a trend at all, so an old archival reading would not say anything useful about a sudden event happening now.

The thresholds below are a physically reasoned first pass, explicitly not backed by a published calibration dataset the way the lake growth thresholds are. No public equivalent of the GLO dataset exists yet for short-window terminus ice-cover volatility in the Himalaya. This factor is live anyway, as an MVP, on the reasoning that a provisional signal clearly labeled as provisional is more useful than no signal at all. That caveat is meant to be impossible to miss.

Adjusting for size

A larger lake or glacier shaking under the same earthquake, or sitting under the same landslide, involves a lot more mass in motion than a small one does, so both the earthquake and landslide components are scaled by the point's real surface area or glacier area. That scaling ranges from 0.5 for anything very small or of unknown size up to a full 1.0 once the area reaches 10 square kilometers or more.

How the data actually gets gathered

Part of this pipeline runs continuously and part of it is a one time import, and the distinction matters for how much you should trust the freshness of any given number.

  • Refreshed continuously. USGS earthquake and landslide detections update every 10 minutes. OpenWeatherMap readings update every 30 minutes for every monitored point. Sentinel-2 satellite readings for every lake and glacier update roughly every 5 days, matching the real revisit cadence. The full risk score for all 220 sites gets recalculated every 30 minutes as that new data comes in.
  • Imported once, static until manually updated. ICIMOD's glacial lake inventory, filtered down to 97 lakes within Nepal, and the Randolph Glacier Inventory version 7, filtered to 123 glacier watch points with a slope over 30 degrees sitting within 10 kilometers of a known river town. Neither of these updates on its own. If ICIMOD or the RGI Consortium publish a revised dataset, the import needs to be re-run by hand.

Assumptions

Every model simplifies reality. These are the specific simplifications this one makes, stated plainly rather than left implicit. A formula built entirely from hand-picked numbers with no grounding is not trustworthy, so Table 6 lays out where every hard-coded threshold in this formula actually comes from: a specific cited study where one exists, or an honest label of "engineering judgment" where it does not.

  • River basins are approximated by a small curated list of towns per basin, not real watershed boundary polygons. A landslide is matched to the basin of the nearest point on the curated river course through those towns.
  • The monsoon seasonal curve is a single calendar-based function applied identically to every lake in the country, regardless of local elevation, aspect, or microclimate.
  • The earthquake decay half-life (12 days) is a hand-chosen middle ground informed by the aftershock literature, not fit to Nepal-specific seismic data.
  • Satellite readings sample a small fixed bounding box (about 1.3km across) around each point's coordinates, not a true lake or glacier polygon. This is a proxy for the real extent, not a measurement of it.
  • The NDWI > 0.1 (water) and NDSI > 0.4 (snow/ice) classification thresholds are standard values from the remote sensing literature. Neither one has been independently validated against ground truth for these specific Himalayan lakes and glaciers.
  • The glacier ice-drop thresholds are physically reasoned from how a structural collapse should look different from seasonal melt (fast vs. slow), not statistically derived from observed collapse events, because no such event has occurred at a tracked glacier since this factor went live.
  • Because the glacier satellite baseline starts today rather than from archival history, a "sudden drop" can currently only be detected relative to what this app itself has observed since deployment, not relative to any earlier real-world event.

Constraints and what this cannot do yet

These are listed plainly here rather than left for someone to discover the hard way while relying on the app.

  • The app's own weather history only goes back to early September 2026. Any question about rainfall before that date has no real data behind it, and any test involving an older event has to say so honestly rather than quietly assume typical conditions.
  • There is no true computer-vision detection of visual debris, scarring, or collapse imagery. "Landslide-type" glacier scoring is currently proxied entirely by the ice/snow-index sudden-drop signal above, not by any image-recognition model looking at the actual satellite scene.
  • Satellite readings are cloud-limited. A cloudy stretch over a lake or glacier for more than the ~15-day glacier comparison window, or below the 30% valid-pixel floor for either factor, simply produces no usable comparison until the sky clears, not a false negative flagged as such elsewhere on the dashboard.
  • The free-tier Sentinel Hub quota (10,000 processing units and 10,000 requests per month) caps how often 220 points can realistically be checked. The ~5-day cadence is a deliberate fit to that budget, which means a structural event faster than that cadence could be missed until the next pass.
  • There is no independent cross-check (for example, Sentinel-1 SAR, which sees through cloud) to confirm an optical ice-drop reading. A false drop caused by unusual cloud or shadow contamination that fools the scene-classification mask cannot currently be caught by a second, independent sensor.
  • The glacier ice-drop threshold has not been tested against any real terminus collapse, because none has occurred at a tracked glacier since the factor went live on September 7, 2026.
  • The seasonal curve and the basin matching used for the landslide floor are both real, defensible approximations, not survey grade measurements. That is stated plainly here rather than letting the numbers imply more precision than they have.
  • A lake can sit at Watch purely because of its static ICIMOD classification and the time of year, with nothing new actually happening. That kind of steady, predictable seasonal signal is kept separate from an actual detected trigger, like fresh heavy rain, a nearby landslide, or a satellite signal, so the dashboard's main alert banner reflects genuinely new information rather than restating a fact you could look up once in the ICIMOD database and never need to check again.

Future work

Roughly in priority order:

  1. Sentinel-1 SAR cross-check for the glacier ice-drop signal. Radar sees through cloud and is not fooled by shadow the way an optical scene-classification mask can be. Requiring agreement between an optical drop and a radar-detected surface change before escalating would meaningfully cut the false-positive risk on the least-validated part of this formula.
  2. Real lake and glacier polygons instead of fixed bounding boxes. ICIMOD and RGI both publish outline geometry in principle; sampling the actual shape instead of a fixed box around the centroid would remove a source of both missed water/ice pixels and contamination from surrounding terrain.
  3. A backtested statistical threshold for glacier ice-drop, once either a real collapse occurs at a tracked point or a published dataset of terminus volatility becomes available, the same way the lake growth threshold was upgraded from a guess to a calibrated value against the GLO dataset.
  4. Automated watershed polygons to replace the curated-town-list basin approximation used for the landslide floor.
  5. A properly fit statistical model for the overall formula weights, if and when enough labeled historical GLOF outcomes in Nepal exist to fit one responsibly. Right now there is not nearly enough labeled event data to do this without overfitting to a handful of cases.

Validating the formula against a real event

Rather than trust the arithmetic alone, the formula was tested against something that actually happened: the ice and rock collapse near Langtang Lirung on August 26, 2026, which dammed the Lhende Khola and sent a flash flood across the border at Rasuwagadhi within about three minutes, reaching the town of Timure roughly thirteen minutes after the initial collapse (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026; Petley, 2026). Three things stand out. First, this location was not sitting at zero beforehand: real, sustained monsoon rainfall and known terrain steepness had already pushed the score into Watch territory at least a week out, well before any landslide signal existed. Second, that week-out Watch is not foresight, it is background susceptibility: steep terrain during a wet monsoon reads the same way whether or not a collapse ever follows, and most of Nepal's steep glacier watch points sit somewhere near this range for most of the monsoon without incident. Third, the landslide detection at 08:37 added a real, additional step on top of that baseline, not a replacement for it, and the score held at that higher level right through the real flood arrival at Timure. This system reacts to what gets detected, it does not forecast a collapse before it happens, and none of the elevated readings before 08:37 should be read as it having done so. Both the strength and the limits of that are shown here rather than smoothed over.
